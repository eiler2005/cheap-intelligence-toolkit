# The three value levers filter

> Practicum for **Chapter 2 of Volume 1**, "The New Price of Intelligence." Time: 10 to 15 minutes.
> Use one worksheet for one task or product. You can also use the
> [interactive browser version](../skills/value-levers-filter/). It does the math in your browser and does not send your data anywhere.

AI makes `output` cheaper. Output is a finished artifact such as a text, report, design, or piece of code. If your value came from producing something quickly and neatly, that advantage is fading. Value is moving to `outcome`: framing the problem, applying judgment, earning trust, reaching people, and taking responsibility for the result. This worksheet is a quick filter. It shows which of the **three levers** creates value in your task and whether cheap AI is wiping out that value.

## The three levers from the chapter

| Lever | What it means | What cheap AI does to it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **New sales and growth** | You do something others cannot do (the "magic") | Helps people who already have something unique, but does not create that uniqueness for them |
| **Cost savings** | You do the same work faster or at a lower cost | **Erases the advantage for everyone.** It becomes the new baseline. |
| **Attention and trust** | People choose you and trust you | Your channel and relationships become more valuable when everyone can produce more content |

> The chapter's point: everyone gets the same cost savings at roughly the same time. An advantage remains where you have **magic**, something others cannot offer, or **attention and trust**, the reason people choose you. This is not a verdict on the task. It tells you where not to place your claim of advantage.

## What gets cheaper and what becomes an advantage

| Gets cheaper (`output`, a commodity) | Becomes an advantage (`outcome`, which you own) |
| --- | --- |
| Standard text, a first draft, or an initial summary | Problem framing and a precise question |
| Analysis based on a standard template | Judgment and taste: what is right or good here |
| Routine code or a standard design | Trust and relationships with specific people |
| Answering a common question | Attention and distribution: who gets chosen, and why |
| Putting data into one format | Responsibility for the result and its consequences |
| Translation, transcription, or formatting | Unique context and data that other people do not have |

## Fill this out for one task

- **Task or product:** `__________________________`
- **Main lever (sales and growth / cost savings / attention and trust):** `____________`
- **Can cheap AI already do this on its own?** (almost completely / partly / no): `____________`
- **Does this set you apart?** (unique / somewhat / same as everyone else): `____________`
- **Your conclusion:** `__________________________`
  - If the lever is cost savings and AI can already do it, the task is becoming a baseline. Do it, but do not expect it to set you apart.
  - If the answer is "same as everyone else" and AI can do it, this is cheaper `output`. Automate it and free your time.
  - If it is unique and the lever is sales and magic, or attention and trust, this is your `outcome`. Strengthen it.
- **One move:** What `output` will you hand to AI, and which `outcome` will you keep and strengthen yourself?
  `__________________________`

This filter does not measure money or calculate return on investment (ROI). It points you in a direction: where value is getting cheaper and where it still belongs to you. Calculate exact savings and revenue separately, using your own data. Use one lever for one task. If the task mixes several levers, split it into parts.

- Sort your week into task buckets and see how much of it is already getting cheaper: [Review your week](week-inventory.md) (Chapter 3).
- Decide where to move your `outcome`: [Your usefulness level](usefulness-levels-self-check.md) (Chapter 5).
- Follow the full route in the [Volume 1 workbook](tom1-workbook.md).
- If you lead a team or a small business, apply the same filter to business functions with the [Small business AI staff map](smb-staff-map.md) (Volume 2).

# Worksheet: inventory one week

> Practicum for the task buckets exercise in Chapter 3 and the three-lever filter in Chapter 2.
> Time: one evening. Use your **calendar and messages from last week**, not your job description. Record what you actually did.

**Faster option:** Use the [interactive browser version](../skills/week-inventory/). Enter your tasks and the tool calculates your exposure. You do not need to install anything, and your data stays on your device.

1. List **10 to 15 tasks** that took up your time last week. Be specific. Do not write "managed projects." Write "collected a contractor status update," "wrote to an unhappy customer," or "prepared a table for management."
2. Put each task in a **bucket**: green, "AI alone"; yellow, "human plus AI"; or red, "human only." Judge the task by what today's tools can do, not by habit. If you are unsure, use the two questions from Chapter 3: Is the result digital? Can the work be done remotely?
3. Assign each task a **lever** from Chapter 2: 1, new revenue; 2, cost savings; 3, attention and trust; or "none" if it has no lever. That kind of task keeps you busy but does not create value.
4. Calculate the **share of your time in the green and yellow buckets**. This is your personal exposure. You are measuring your own week, not the profession as a whole.

**Result:** time in green + yellow = ____% | tasks with no lever = ____

## Three questions to answer afterward

1. Which of my tasks have **already become cheaper**, even if I still do them by hand?
2. Which tasks do I keep because they make me **feel busy**, rather than because they change a result?
3. Which red-bucket tasks are almost missing from my week, **and why**? Does my role lack them, or am I avoiding them?

## Three honest scenarios

- **Put the list away:** A year from now, your week looks the same, but the market values it less.
- **Go faster inside the green bucket:** This helps as a skill but fails as a strategy. Volume grows where the premium is moving toward zero (Chapter 2).
- **Shift the weight:** Set a six-month target of adding **two or three red-bucket tasks to your week**. This takes longer than learning new prompts, but it moves you toward work whose value is rising.

> The full profession map, the Task-to-Outcome Map, appears in Chapter 9 and in the live version of this repository.

 Reassess the buckets every six months because the boundary keeps moving.

# Career ladder self-check

> Practicum for Chapter 4, "The Ladder Breaks: Entry and Mid-Career."
> Practicum URL: `https://cheap-intelligence.vercel.app/en/`.

Goal: In one evening, find the weak point in your career ladder: entry, middle, or top.

## 1. Three questions

| Question | Your answer | What counts as a risk signal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What am I learning through my work now? | | My week has no tasks just above my current level. AI or a senior colleague does all the work that could help me grow. |
| What do I own, and what do I pass along? | | Most of my week goes to status updates, summaries, forwarding information, and checking other people's work. |
| Who learns from my simple tasks? | | Agents already handle all routine work, so beginners have no safe place to practice. |

## 2. Your weak section

Choose one section to focus on for the next 90 days.

| Section | Choose this if | Main move |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Entry | You are looking for a first chance, but there are fewer "simple tasks" available | Build proof of a result instead of waiting to gain seniority |
| Middle | You are no longer a beginner, but you spend much of your time passing along other people's work | Shift your time from forwarding work to owning an outcome |
| Top | You are responsible for people, a team, or hiring | Design an apprenticeship instead of hoping one happens on its own |

## 3. One move this week

Choose one action you can finish within seven days.

| Section | Action |
| --- | --- |
| Entry | Make one small public artifact: an analysis, prototype, template, short research note, or working example |
| Middle | Calculate how much of your week goes to passing work along, then choose one handoff for an agent to handle |
| Top | Find one learning task that you will not automate completely because a person can grow by doing it |

## 4. Check again in one month

- What can I do better now, not just faster?
- Which task has become mine to own?
- Who did I give work that can help them grow?

## 4. Example moves

| A manager collects status updates from ten sources by hand | Move from 2 to 3: an agent prepares a draft, and a human checks the handoffs and exceptions |
| A business owner receives a draft tax return from a service | Move from 1 to 2 or 4: decide who checks the risk and who is responsible to the tax authority |
| A team repeats the same manual check every week | Move from 4 to 5: turn the check into a checklist, template, or tool |

## 5. No polished imitation

The plan does not count if:

- you wrote a new prompt but did not change the checking criteria;
- you made the output faster but did not make the outcome clearer;
- you passed the risk to the model;
- you called it a system, but it does not work without you;
- you do not know how you will tell whether it is better in one month.

## Final formula

**One level. One task. One quarter.**

One honest move per quarter gives you four moves a year. Few people climb this ladder faster.

# Turn a repeated task into a skill

> Practicum for Chapter 6, "The Human as a System." Time: 20 to 30 minutes.
> Continue here after the [Personal Harness Builder](personal-harness-builder.md). Once you can see your harness, package one repeatable part as a portable skill.

## Why this helps

Use a simple rule: **If you explain the same logic to AI for the fourth or fifth time, it is already a skill.** The problem is not that the model is slow to learn. You have not yet built a system for that procedure. Repeating one explanation can cost 8 to 12 minutes a day. Across a month and several tasks, that becomes hours.

A skill is an instruction file called `SKILL.md`. An agent loads it when a task falls within its scope. Write the logic once, then stop explaining it from scratch. You can find skills made by other people in [skill banks](../watch/skill-banks.md). This worksheet helps you make your own.

## Step 1. Find a candidate

Choose a repeated procedure that you keep explaining from the beginning.

| Sign | Your example |
| --- | --- |
| I have explained this to AI at least four times | |
| I explain it a little differently each time, so the result varies | |
| I know what "done correctly" means | |

If all three rows fit, you have a skill candidate. Start with the one you repeat most often.

## Step 2. Describe the procedure in plain language

Answer four questions. Your answers will form the body of the skill.

1. **When to use it.** Which tasks should trigger this skill?
2. **What goes in.** Which materials or data do you provide?
3. **How to do it.** Write the steps you usually explain each time.
4. **What comes out and how to check it.** Define the output format and your verification point.

## Step 3. Build the `SKILL.md` file

Copy and complete this minimal template:

```markdown
name: contract-review
desc: Reviews an incoming contract and lists risks for my side, with a quote from each relevant clause.

## When to use

Use this skill when I provide a contract and ask you to assess its risks.

## Input

The contract text, link, or file.

## Steps

1. Identify risks for MY side. I am the customer.
2. Sort the risks by severity.
3. Quote the relevant clause for each risk.
4. Suggest revised wording.

## Output

Table: risk | severity | clause quote | suggested revision.

I check every quote against the contract and take responsibility for the decision.
```

## Step 4. Put it where the agent can find it

- Add the file to the skills folder used by your AI tool or agent. Claude Code, Codex, Cursor, and other tools use different paths.
- Give the file a clear name. One job belongs in one skill.

## Step 5. Test and update it

- Run the skill on one or two real tasks. If the result varies, make the steps more precise.
- Review it once a month. Remove what is outdated and add what is missing.
- **Access and security:** A skill that can reach email, files, or money should require confirmation and run in a [sandbox](personal-harness-builder.md). Treat someone else's skill as you would treat someone else's code. See [trusting a skill](../watch/skill-banks.md).

## What you get

Your list of skills becomes a portable part of your harness. When you start an agent, it can load the right skill for the task. The irritation of explaining the same thing again is useful evidence. It points to the next skill you should write.

# Tools map by layer

> Practicum for Chapter 6, "The Human as a System."
> Update this page when new evidence appears. The selection principles stay stable, while examples change.

**Important:** Chapter 6 avoids naming specific tools because they change quickly. This page is the
living version. It gives selection principles for each layer and dated examples. The examples show a
class of tools and are not recommendations. The [Tools Map](../watch/tools-map.md) lists concrete
options for every layer, and the card for your profession lists tools for that role in the
[catalog](../applications/_index.md).

---

## How to read this map

Each layer of the personal AI system has one selection question, a short set of criteria, and dated
examples of a tool class.

Ask three questions before adding anything to your system:

1. Can I explain this tool's role in the system?
2. Does it solve a task I have already identified in the TOM?
3. Will I use it regularly, at least once a week?

If the answer is no, you do not need the tool now.

---

## Layer 1: Thinking

**Selection question:** Does the tool help me think with AI while keeping my own judgment active?

**Criteria:**

- Supports a conversation instead of one request followed by one answer.
- Holds a long context without losing the thread.
- Lets you ask follow-up questions and change direction.

**Tool class (examples, 2025-2026):**

- Long-context chat: Claude, GPT-4o, Gemini Advanced for iterative reasoning.
- Conversation workspaces: Claude Projects and ChatGPT Projects for persistent topic context.
- Native voice mode when thinking on the move matters more than typing.

**Sign of a weak Thinking layer:** you ask AI one question, copy the answer, and stop.

---

## Layer 2: Knowledge

**Selection question:** Can I give the AI tool enough task context within two minutes?

**Criteria:**

- Stores notes in a form you can copy into an AI chat.
- Searches content rather than only tags.
- Keeps data somewhere you control, including outside company systems.

**Tool class (examples, 2025-2026):**

- Searchable personal knowledge bases: Obsidian, a personal Notion space, Bear.
- Vector search across your documents: NotebookLM and Mem.ai for questions about your own corpus.
- Structured notes with backlinks: Logseq and Roam Research for nonlinear connections.

**Sign of a weak Knowledge layer:** every AI task starts with more than 20 minutes of searching for
the right material.

---

## Layer 3: Tools

**Selection question:** How many tools do I use actively, and can I explain the role of each one?

**Criteria for every tool:**

- I use it at least once a week.
- I can explain why it is better than an alternative for a specific task.
- It saves at least 30 minutes a week or makes something possible that I could not do before.

**Basic roles, meaning functions rather than products (2025-2026):**

- Capable chat and reasoning for difficult analysis, synthesis, and planning.
- Search with summaries for research, such as Perplexity or Gemini with search.
- A specialist agent for recurring work in code, law, finance, or another field.
- Workflow automation that connects tools when a task repeats more than five times a week.

**Sign of a weak Tools layer:** you pay for more than 15 subscriptions and still keep looking for
something new.

---

## Layer 4: Proof

**Selection question:** Where can someone outside my team see my work?

**Criteria:**

- The artifact is available without registration or behind only a small barrier.
- It shows your way of thinking as well as the final result.
- You updated it within the past six months.

**Tool class (examples, 2025-2026):**

- Public writing: Substack, Medium, or a Telegram channel with an archive.
- Professional portfolios: LinkedIn with cases rather than only job titles, and GitHub.
- Video and audio: short explanations of difficult ideas through YouTube Shorts or podcasts.
- Repositories and templates: open materials that other people use.

**Sign of a weak Proof layer:** only people who worked with you directly know about your expertise.

---

## Layer 5: Trust

**Selection question:** Does the process include a consistent review before the result moves forward?

**Criteria:**

- Review is part of the workflow, not something you do "when there is time."
- It checks the mistakes that AI tends to make in your field.
- It takes less than 15% of the task's total time.

**Verification principles (2025-2026):**

- Check numbers and statistics manually against a primary source rather than through AI.
- Check legal and financial conclusions against current rules, not the model's training data.
- For professional recommendations, ask what has changed since 2024. Many models have a knowledge
  cutoff.

**Sign of a weak Trust layer:** you pass along the AI result without reading or checking it.

---

## Layer 6: Outcome

**Selection question:** Is my work connected to a measurable result for the customer?

**Criteria:**

- I know the metric that should change for the customer after my work.
- I learn whether the customer used the result.
- One month later, I can say what changed.

**Measurement tools (examples, 2025-2026):**

- Direct metrics: OKRs or KPIs in a personal tracker, Notion, or a quarterly results table.
- Indirect metrics: customer feedback and links to your work in other people's documents.
- Portfolio tracking: compare tasks in the TOM after three months and see what changed in the "next
  level" column.

**Sign of a weak Outcome layer:** you can describe what you did but cannot say what changed.

---

## How to update this map

- Review named tools every six months.
- Keep the principles and criteria stable unless the nature of a layer changes.
- Update the version date whenever the examples change.
- Do not add a tool until you have tested it in a real work task.

[Examples by Level](../harness-examples/examples-by-level.md).

# Prompt bank: useful friction instead of a finished answer

> Practicum for Chapter 8, "Cognitive Debt," and Volume 2, Chapter 7, "Children."
> This is **not** a list of 100 prompts. It is one principle with examples. The left column hands the thinking to the machine. The right column keeps some effort with you. Each pair explains what it trains.

## Core examples from Chapter 8

| Hands over the skill | Trains the skill | Why it trains |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Write a reply to the customer" | "Give me three tone options and the tradeoffs of each. I will choose." | You keep judgment and taste |
| "Draw a conclusion from this table" | "Show me two hypotheses and where the data does not support them" | You check instead of accepting the answer |
| "Solve the problem" | "Explain two approaches. I will choose one and finish it." | You remain the author of the solution |
| "Write the code" | "Sketch the structure and mark the places where errors are likely" | You understand what you run |
| "Write the strategy" | "Ask me five questions that must be answered before a strategy can be built" | The thinking stays with you |

## More examples by task type

This section changes as the tools change.

**Writing and analysis:** Use "Challenge my draft as a skeptic" instead of "Rewrite this for me." Use "Which sources could disprove this?" instead of "Confirm this."

**Code:** Use "Where will this break under load?" instead of "Optimize it." Use "Explain this line" instead of "Just fix it."

**Decisions and strategy:** Use "Argue against my plan" instead of "Approve it." Use "What did I miss?" instead of "Make a plan."

**Learning something new:** Use "Give me a problem that checks whether I understood" instead of "Explain it again." Or say, "Guide me with questions. Do not give me the answer right away."

Every useful prompt should say **which effort stays with the human**. If the answer is "none," you have not added friction. You have only asked for the same finished answer in different words. Add your best new prompts at the top and include a short note explaining what each one trains.

## Choose a capability to practice: Naval's four pillars

The hardest field is "Capability I will keep and practice myself." When AI makes production cheaper, skills that anyone can quickly hire or copy lose some of their advantage. Investor Naval Ravikant described four pillars that have become even more useful in the AI era. Treat them as a guide for choosing a capability to protect and practice, not as a secret formula for success.

- **Specific knowledge.** You cannot learn it from a standard textbook or hire an identical replacement on the market. It grows from your path, curiosity, and unusual mix of interests. The more specific it is, the harder it is to copy. To practice it, follow your real interests instead of someone else's list of skills "in demand."
- **Judgment.** When leverage such as AI is cheap and available to almost everyone, the amount of work matters less than the quality of the decisions about where to use it and where not to trust it. Practice judgment at the AI-free checkpoints that you keep for yourself during days 1 to 30.
- **Building and selling.** One capability creates the result. The other gets it to the people who need it. Together they can produce an outsized return. AI can support both, but does not replace either one.
- **Accountability.** Leverage and trust go to the person who takes a visible risk and puts their name behind the result, not simply to the person who works the most hours. This connects directly to the evidence you will collect during days 31 to 60: a result under your own name.

This is one investor's view, not research or a guarantee of income. Use the structure without borrowing anyone else's numbers or promises. ## Where the Leverage multiplier comes from

The idea comes from investor and entrepreneur Naval Ravikant's 2018 thread, "How to Get Rich (without getting lucky)," and Eric Jorgenson's 2020 book *The Almanack of Naval Ravikant*, available at navalmanack.com. Naval describes three forms of leverage:

- **Labor:** Hiring and delegating to other people.
- **Capital:** Money that can keep working.
- **Permissionless leverage:** Code and media. They can be copied at almost no cost, and you do not need someone's approval to use them, unlike labor and capital.

# The five deficits audit (Practicum for "When Intelligence Became Cheap")

📋 A ready-made skill for an AI assistant. Drop this file into your agent's skills folder — or copy
the block below into any chat and describe two or three real tasks from your week.

The worksheet this comes from, for filling in by hand:
[The five deficits audit](../../playbooks/five-deficits-audit.md).

```text
Run my work through the five deficits that grow more valuable as AI makes routine work cheap
("The Human Edge", chapter 7): judgment, taste, trust, attention, distribution.

Collect from me if I have not given it:
1. Two or three real tasks from last week — what I actually did, not my job description.
2. How each one ended: who accepted the result and what happened next.

What to do:
1. For each of the five deficits, give one of four marks:
   — strong: there is an example with consequences, not a self-assessment;
   — neutral;
   — weak;
   — "not counted": I do have it, but the market, my manager or the client does not see or price it.
2. Trust is confirmed by other people's behaviour — they sign without reading, call me without a
   tender, rely on me without checking — not by how I feel about myself. Hold to that rule.
3. Do not give me three moves. Pick ONE deficit where a move pays off most over the coming quarter.
4. Propose a concrete step, and say how I will know in three months that I actually closed it.

Discipline:
- Mark by evidence, not by how I describe myself. No example means "not counted" — say so.
- Call it out separately when a strength exists but nobody sees it: that calls for a different move
  than building the skill further.
- No "develop your taste" without specifics. The step has to be something I can start this week.
- Do not invent studies, numbers or courses. If unsure, say "check this".

Answer format:
1. Table: deficit | mark | what the mark rests on.
2. The one deficit chosen, and why that one.
3. The concrete step for the quarter.
4. The three-month check: what has to change for this to count as closed.
```
